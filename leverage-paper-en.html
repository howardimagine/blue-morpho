Conditional Leverage Rotation: Rules, Inference, and Implementation Boundaries for Using Trend and Realized Volatility to Determine Leveraged Exposure to US Stock Indices

Blue Morpho Research Report ("Ridewell" Project) · 2nd Edition · September 28, 2026
Data period 2000-03-08 to 2026-09-25 (daily) · Out-of-sample supplement 1966–1999 · Code stock-radar/agents/lev_rotation.py(Version ed91e33 onward)

Executive Summary

  • Strategy.Signals computed from 1x ETF close prices, with next-day open adjustment to {cash, 1x, 2x, 3x} real leveraged ETF exposure. Three legs: Nasdaq 100 (QQQ, up to 3x, volatility target), S&P 500 (SPY, up to 3x, 200-day MA plus CPI cap), Philadelphia Semiconductor (SOXX, up to 2x, volatility threshold, 10-day cooldown, 30% permanent 1x). Recommended equal-weight three legs, monthly rebalance.
  • Performance (2000-03 to 2026-09, net of fees and costs, zero interest on cash).Three-leg equal weight: annualized 19.2%, annualized volatility 28.3%, Sharpe 0.70, max drawdown −36.5%; over the same period, three-leg 1x equal weight: annualized 9.8%, Sharpe 0.42, max drawdown −73.6%. Last 15 years: 31.1% vs 21.7%.
  • Inference strength: evidence for returns, not for efficiency.After correcting for autocorrelation and fat tails (Newey-West HAC, stationary block bootstrap), the three-leg equal-weight average excess return is significant both for the full period and the last 15 years (bootstrap p = 0.001, 0.008); the SOX single leg is not significant. But the 90% interval for the Sharpe difference includes 0, and is slightly negative for the last 15 years — the excess return mainly comes from taking on more leverage. The probability of a shallower drawdown is 0.94 (median 21 points shallower), which is the most reliable effect of the rule. Deflated Sharpe (127 trials): 98–100%.
  • Excess return sources are concentrated.Excess return mainly comes from exiting during the three bear markets of 2000–02, 2008, 2022, and being fully invested during 2013, 2017, 2019–21, 2023; the strategy systematically lags in non-bear, low-trend years (2004–06, 2011, 2015). The rule is essentially long "trend continuation with slow declines" market structure.
  • External validity is limited.Applying the same rule across 38 markets with leveraged products, only tech stocks, S&P, and Nasdaq pass; Nikkei 225 has consistently lagged 1x since 1990 (including the 2011–2026 bull market). There is no reliable leading signal before structural changes.
  • Forward-looking.Using 1-year block bootstrap with 4,000 ten-year paths, at 50% confidence in backtested excess returns, the three-leg equal-weight ten-year annualized median is 15.2% (5th percentile 1.1%, 95th percentile 31.0%), with about a 4% probability of a ten-year loss; under the same conditions, holding three-leg 1x continuously gives a median of 10.6%, with an 11% loss probability.
  • Implementation.Annual turnover is about 15.7 times (combined across three legs), concentrated at the open; TQQQ and SOXL median daily trading volumes are about $4.8 billion and $5.8 billion respectively, UPRO about $390 million — capacity is not a constraint for individual to mid-size accounts. Main implementation risks are product liquidation, regulatory restrictions, and the impact of 23-hour trading starting 2026-12 on the "next-day open" assumption.

1. Research Question and Contribution

The long-term returns of daily-rebalanced L-times leveraged ETFs are eroded by realized variance. Using this study's data, holding synthetic 3x Nasdaq 100 continuously since March 2000 results in a terminal value of 0.45x principal, synthetic 3x Philadelphia Semiconductor falls below 0.02x, while 1x versions are 7.9x and 13.7x respectively. Existing literature suggests that if leverage is used only during low volatility, upward-trending periods, expected returns can turn positive [4–8]. This study examines whether this proposition holds under the following live-trading constraints: (i) only actually available product multiples can be held; (ii) signals are computed at close and executed at the next day's open, with overnight gaps borne by the old exposure; (iii) leveraged ETFs use their actual prices after listing; (iv) cash earns no interest, while financing costs are fully deducted.

This study's contribution lies not in the rule components (all found in the literature), but in: reconstructing the backtest under live-trading constraints; conducting adversarial validation at the statistical, execution, and structural levels, with unsupported results explicitly reported; proposing a ten-year forward-looking distribution and parameterizing "confidence in the backtest"; and enumerating failure modes via scenario analysis while testing the leading nature of early signals.

2. Literature Positioning

Time-series momentum and trend filters.Faber [1] showed that a 10-month moving average filter produces returns close to buy-and-hold while greatly reducing drawdowns; Moskowitz, Ooi, and Pedersen [2] documented time-series momentum and its positive returns during crises across 58 futures markets; Hurst, Ooi, and Pedersen [3] extended the analysis back to 1880, noting that a single market or single decade may fail entirely — a point directly validated in Section 7.4 of this study.

Path dependency of leveraged ETFs.Cheng and Madhavan [4], Avellaneda and Zhang [5] give the approximate long-term return of daily-rebalanced products as g(L) ≈ L·μ − ½·L²·σ² − (L−1)·rf − fees, with optimal leverage L* ≈ (μ − rf)/σ². Using full-period QQQ estimates (μ ≈ 10%, σ ≈ 23%), L* ≈ 1.9, meaning unconditional 3x exceeds the optimal value. The economic function of the rule is to make σ a conditional variable: at 20-day realized volatility of 17%, with the same μ, L* approaches 3.5.

Volatility management.Moreira and Muir [6] proved that scaling exposure inversely with prior realized variance improves Sharpe ratio, because risk premiums do not rise proportionally during high-volatility periods; Harvey et al. [7] found the effect significant in equities and credit but weak in bonds and commodities, consistent with the cross-market results in Section 7.3 of this study. Gayed [8]'s backtest combining a 200-day MA with leverage on the S&P 500 forms the rule skeleton of this project.

Backtest credibility.This study uses Bailey and López de Prado's deflated Sharpe ratio [9], Bailey et al.'s Combinatorially Symmetric Cross-Validation (CSCV) for backtest overfitting probability [10], referencing Harvey, Liu, and Zhu [11]'s argument on multiple-testing thresholds, as well as McLean and Pontiff [12]'s estimate of about half the return decay after publication.Position SizingReferencing Kelly [13], Thorp [14], MacLean, Thorp, and Ziemba [15].

3.2 Return Construction

Let rt be the close-to-close return of the 1x underlying on day t, gt be the gap of day t's open relative to the previous day's close, rf,t be the daily short rate, f be the annual fee, and c be the one-way cost. Synthetic L-times daily return:

R(L)_t = L·r_t − (L−1)·r_f,t − 𝟙[L>1]·f/252 (L ≥ 1) R(L)_t = L·r_t (L < 1, remaining balance is zero-interest cash)

Signal xt is determined after the close on day t; day t+1's account return is split into an overnight segment (old exposure) and an intraday segment (new exposure), net of turnover costs:

1 + R_t+1 = [1 + G(x_t−1)_t+1] · [1 + R(x_t)_t+1] / [1 + G(x_t)_t+1] − c·|x_t − x_t−1|

where G(L) is the overnight gap of the L-times product (using actual open prices after listing). Fractional exposures (such as 0.3 or 1.7 for SOXX) are achieved via cash-weighted combinations of 1x and L-times products. Annualized returns are calculated by calendar years (not trading days ÷ 252, as the latter overstates Taiwan stocks with 245 days by about 0.6 points).

3.3 Look-Ahead Bias Control

Self-check (tests/test_lev_rotation.py) runs three adversarial tests for each rule: (a) replace prices after day K with a random sequence, verifying that exposure before day K is completely unchanged; (b) verify that day K+1's return depends only on exposure determined before day K; (c) plant a known "signal-day close execution" cheat and confirm it can be detected. Macro data is aligned by publication delay (CPI +45 days; VIX and spreads delayed by one day). Incomplete intraday-captured K bars are removed before calculation.

6. Statistical Inference

6.1 Why an ordinary t-test doesn't fit

The most common approach is a one-sample t-test on monthly excess return (rule − same-leg 1x). For this study, it fails in four ways, and the direction of the failure is inconsistent, so simply 'raising the threshold' won't fix it:

  • i.i.d. does not hold.The excess return from leverage rotation comes from position state, and state persists for months; the first-order autocorrelation of monthly excess return is −0.05 to −0.20, and volatility also clusters with market conditions. The standard error of the ordinary t-test is therefore biased (in this sample, negative autocorrelation actuallyoverstatesthe standard error, understating t).
  • Fat tails and skew.Monthly excess return kurtosis is 2.9–5.7, skew is −1.0 to +0.3; 319 months is not enough for the central limit theorem to fully smooth out the tails.
  • Excess return is concentrated in a few episodes.The 3 years with the largest excess return contribute 45–58% of full-period excess (99% for the SOX leg). The effective independent observations are 'a handful of bear markets,' not 319 months, so the nominal sample size of a mean test overstates the information content.
  • Asking the wrong question.The strategy's claim isn't just 'earns more on average,' but 'earns more while suffering less severe damage.' A mean test can't answer whether risk-adjusted performance is better or whether drawdown is shallower. Also, the rule was selected from 127 candidates, and a single test's p-value doesn't account for that selection.

What was tested.

  • H1 holds (combined, QQQ, SPY); SOX does not.After correcting for autocorrelation and fat tails, the three-leg combination's average excess return is significant both full-period and over the last 15 years (bootstrap p ≤ 0.008); QQQ and SPY are significant full-period; SOX is marginal full-period (p = 0.06) with no evidence over the last 15 years. The ordinary t-test isleans conservativeon this data.
  • H2 does not hold.The 90% interval for the Sharpe difference includes 0 for every series, and is even skewed negative over the last 15 years (SPY's interval lies entirely below 0). The excess return is mainlythe payoff for taking on more leverage risk, not an improvement in risk-adjusted efficiency. This is the most important — and most easily misread — conclusion of this study.
  • H3 largely holds.The three-leg combination and SOX show a shallower drawdown in 94–95% of resampled paths, with a median shallowing of about 20 points; QQQ only 66%, so the protection of a single leg isn't reliable. The rule's value is concentrated in the tail, not the average.
  • H4 partially holds.The combination won 18 out of 26 years (p = 0.038), and 11/14 over the last 15 years; but the top three years still contribute 46–63% of excess return, so the effective sample size is far smaller than the number of months.
  • H5 see 6.4.The direction still holds after deflation; the SOX leg shows the most evident selection component.

Combined, the claim should read:On U.S. large-cap tech indices, this set of rules lets an investor hold higher average leverage at a drawdown tolerance close to 1x; it increases returns, but there's no evidence it increases Sharpe.

Limits of the tests.

  • Stationarity assumption.The bootstrap can only reshuffle episodes that already happened. A regime like Japan's post-1990 'long-term failure to return above the moving average' — if it never appeared in the sample — cannot be produced by any resampling (see §7 adversarial validation).
  • Parameter choice.Block length (12 months, 1 year) and the HAC lag (Bartlett, 4–5 periods) affect the p-values; rerunning with 6- or 24-month blocks keeps the combination's and QQQ's p-values below 0.03, so the conclusion is unchanged; marginal series shift: SPY's last-15-years p is 0.056 / 0.038 / 0.021 (6 / 12 / 24-month blocks), SOX's full-period p is 0.067 / 0.064 / 0.052, never passing 0.05.
  • Multiple comparisons not fully addressed.Table 6b's p-values are for a single already-selected rule; the selection effect is only approximated via DSR, without a full White Reality Check or Hansen SPA across the whole candidate set. Under the Harvey, Liu & Zhu [11] t > 3 threshold, none of the rows pass.
  • Statistical power.The intervals for both the Sharpe difference and drawdown difference are wide; 'not rejected' doesn't mean 'no difference' — it means 26 years of data isn't enough to distinguish.
  • In-sample.All tests are run on the same historical period used to design the rule; true out-of-sample data is limited to 1966–99 (no actual product existed) and the period since launch.

Resampling the entire daily return series 3,000 times with 1-year blocks gives a 90% interval of [+3.7%, +13.2%] for the three-leg equal-weight annualized log excess return (QQQ [+3.1%, +17.4%]), consistent with Table 6b: the direction is credible, the magnitude is uncertain.

7.2 Out-of-sample: 1966–1999

A synthetic series was reconstructed using ^GSPC (from 1966), ^IXIC (from 1972), ^NDX (from 1986) price indices plus a fixed dividend (3%, 1%, 1%) and ^IRX financing cost, with rule parameters unchanged. S&P T3 3x annualized 15.7% (1x 11.5%); −44% during 1973–74 (3x buy-and-hold −86%); −33% in 1987 (−70%); full-period drawdown −53%. Nasdaq V2_50 3x annualized 26.4% (1x 15.1%). However, during NDX's 1986–2000 one-sided bull market, V2_50 3x annualized 29.1% is lower than 1x's 30.6% and has a deeper drawdown; during the S&P's 1966–82 sixteen-year sideways period, the 3x rule annualized 4.4–6.3%, comparable to 1x's 5.6%.

7.3 Cross-market transplant

The five rules were applied unadjusted to 38 leveraged-product U.S. sector, country, region, commodity, and bond ETFs. Only XLK, SPY, and QQQ pass four or more criteria (last-15-years annualized beats 1x, and full-period drawdown no more than 5 points deeper than 1x); XLI passes three; XLF, SMH, SOXX pass two; the remaining 31 (including IWM, DIA, MDY, XLV, EFA, EEM, FXI, EWJ, EWY, GLD, TLT, XLE) pass one or fewer. Common traits of the passing group: the 1x version using only the MA200 filter nearly matches buy-and-hold (−2.6 vs. −4.2 points); 20-day volatility correlates more strongly with the next 20-day volatility (0.70 vs. 0.65); overnight gap accounts for a smaller share of daily variance (35% vs. 42%; 50–70% for emerging markets and single countries); long-term annualized return is higher (10.4% vs. 7.9%).

7.4 Control country: Nikkei 225

V2_50 3x annualized 24.9% in 1971–89 (1x 18.1%); −7.8% in 1990–2010 (1x −5.2%, 3x buy-and-hold −34.6%); −2.5% in 2011–26 (1x +12.3%). In 1989, Japan had a structural narrative similar to today's U.S. tech sector (index constituent turnover, world's second-largest market, technological leadership), and the rule would have passed every criterion in this paper at that time; after the structural shift, the rule kept lagging 1x even during subsequent bull periods.This is the most important risk identified in this study, and one that cannot be eliminated by backtesting.

7.5 Single-day gap injection

A −20% overnight gap was injected on any fully-invested 3x trading day over the last 15 years (3x would suffer −60%), taking the worst-case timing: QQQ's last-15-years annualized return goes from 37.0% → 28.9%, drawdown −63%, still above 1x's 20.1%. A single 1987-type event does not destroy the rule; two in a row would.

8. Forward-looking distribution: ten-year horizon

Using 1-year-block bootstrap of full-period daily returns, 4,000 ten-year paths were generated to obtain the annualized return distribution. The bootstrap addresses 'the same rule, different luck'; it does not address selection bias — the rule was chosen from 127 combinations, so the backtest average itself may be biased upward. A confidence parameter c ∈ [0, 1] is therefore introduced, shifting each path's log annualized return by −(1 − c)·ε̂, where ε̂ is the sample log annualized excess return (8.2 points for the three-leg equal weight). c = 0 is equivalent to assuming the rule has no excess return; c = 0.5 corresponds to the commonly cited post-publication decay [12] and multiple-testing discount [11] in the literature.

9. Implementation Considerations

9.1 Capacity and Liquidity

Median daily trading value over the trailing 252 trading days: TQQQ ~US$4.8 billion (minimum US$1.4 billion), SOXL ~US$5.8 billion (minimum US$1.4 billion), SOXX ~US$2.8 billion (minimum US$500 million), UPRO ~US$390 million (minimum US$170 million). Capping single-turnover at no more than 1% of average daily volume, the UPRO leg can accommodate roughly US$4 million, while the TQQQ and SOXL legs can accommodate roughly US$48–58 million. Turnover concentrates at the opening auction and the first 30 minutes after open; similar trend-following rules (MA200 being the most common signal) may exit on the same day, so actual slippage on stress days may exceed the ×3 scenario in Section 7.1.

9.2 Taxes and Costs (Taiwan Residents)

Capital gains on U.S. ETFs are not subject to U.S. tax for Taiwan residents; overseas income is included in the Basic Income Amount (with an annual exemption of NT$7.5 million). Dividends are subject to 30% withholding, but leveraged ETF distributions are low, adding less than 0.1 point/year of impact, which this study does not account for. Brokerage fees and minimum charges for sub-brokered accounts may push the effective one-way cost above 0.10% for small accounts.

9.3 Product and Regulatory Risk

Issuers may liquidate leveraged products after extreme events (as with several oil and volatility products in 2020); being liquidated while holding a position forces an exit at the worst possible time. The SEC proposed restricting retail access to leveraged and inverse funds in 2020 (later withdrawn); sub-brokerage firms may delist specific products. These risks cannot be mitigated by rules — only managed through position sizing.

9.4 Market Structure: 23-Hour Trading

Nasdaq targets continuous trading from Sunday 21:00 to Friday 20:00 (ET) starting 2026-12-06, with only a 20:00–21:00 daily pause; NYSE Arca has already been approved for 22-hour trading [18]. The regular 16:00 close and closing auction remain unchanged, so signal calculation is unaffected; however, "next-day open" execution may shift earlier to that evening's 21:00 overnight session. Overnight liquidity is expected to be only a few percent of daytime volume, and leveraged ETF overnight quotes may deviate from NAV. We recommend tracking overnight spreads and volume for three months after go-live, backtesting the actual cost of "16:00 signal → 21:00 execution," before deciding whether to adjust.

9.5 Operations

Signals are computed on a schedule after each daily close; close confirmations are pushed one hour after close, execution reminders are pushed thirty minutes before open, and weekly exposure is pushed every Monday. System state, secrets, and data have established version control, off-site/offline archiving, and machine-migration procedures (Appendix C). In-house execution slippage is not currently tracked systematically; we recommend recording the opening reference price and actual execution price at every turnover.

10. Failure Modes & Monitoring

The rule depends on five assumptions: A) the underlying follows a long trend with slow declines; B) volatility clusters and is predictable from a 20-day window; C) the product exists, is liquid, and tracks accurately; D) long-term drift is no less than roughly 10% and financing costs remain reasonable; E) a single daily close signal is sufficient. 11. Limitations

  • Synthetic data.The 2–3x returns during the 2000–02 and 2008 crashes are synthetic and do not incorporate rising financing costs, premium/discount effects, or liquidity breakdowns during extreme periods. The rule's conclusion of "avoiding the crash" rests mainly on synthetic series.
  • Few independent samples.26 years contain only two to three complete cycles; rolling ten-year windows overlap heavily, leaving roughly two truly independent ten-year samples. All win rates and distributions should be treated as order-of-magnitude estimates.
  • In-sample design.The rule's components are drawn from post-1990s literature; 1999–2016 cannot be regarded as true out-of-sample. Genuine out-of-sample periods are only 1966–99 (no actual product) and post-2017.
  • Significance.The average excess return is significant at the portfolio level (HAC, bootstrap), but does not meet the t > 3 multiple-testing threshold, and no SPA test was performed; the Sharpe difference is not significant, and the drawdown improvement is the strongest evidence.
  • Concentration.All three legs are US large-cap tech-related indices, correlated at 0.56–0.74; not comparable to the cross-market diversification found in the literature spanning dozens of markets.
  • SOXX leg.Parameter cloud, execution stress, and the recent t-value (0.34) all indicate this leg has the thinnest margin; leverage is retained per investor preference, and its size should be the smallest of the three legs.
  • Behavioral cost.The rule calls for buying back leverage at fear peaks such as March 2009, April 2020, and January 2023; the worst three-year period trailed 1x by about 15 points. The backtest does not account for this cost.

12. Conclusion

Under live-trading constraints, leverage rotation—using trend to decide market presence and realized volatility to decide the multiplier—has produced excess returns since 2000 on US large-cap tech indices that are directionally credible but uncertain in magnitude: the three-leg equal-weight portfolio achieves Sharpe 0.70 vs. 0.42, halves the maximum drawdown, and shows an annualized log excess return with a 90% interval of [+3.7%, +13.2%]. However, the excess return mainly comes from taking on more leverage rather than a higher Sharpe ratio; the average excess return does not meet the multiple-testing threshold; over the past 15 years risk-adjusted returns are similar to passive holding; and the rule passes in only high-drift, trend-persistent, slow-decline US large-cap tech among 38 markets, while continuing to fail in Japan since 1990.

We therefore position this strategy as conditional leverage on the structural pattern of "sustained US large-cap tech bull markets," rather than as alpha independent of market structure. Our recommendation for ten-year allocators is: use the three-leg equal-weight approach rather than a single bet; use the forward-looking distribution at c = 0.5 (ten-year annualized median around 15%, 5th percentile around 1%) as the planning baseline rather than backtest figures; limit the leverage layer's size to a range that can withstand a single-day −50% drop and three consecutive years of trailing 1x; and continue monitoring volatility predictability and product risk. Position sizing matters more than rule parameters.
